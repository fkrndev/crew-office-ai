# Lead Magnet Builder

A lead magnet is not a PDF you gate behind a form. It is a piece of genuinely pay-worthy value, handed to a stranger for free, because the free value IS your risk reversal when the service itself cannot carry one. Done right, it is the reason a cold prospect replies to someone they have never heard of. Done wrong (thin, generic, effort-signalling), it actively tells the prospect what working with you would feel like.

This skill designs the asset and writes its two email framings, including the one that outperforms everything else in my testing when there is a real artifact behind it: the Built-For-You.

## When to use

- `/offer-framing` ran the decision stack and landed on step 2 (no service front-end available)
- An angle on the `/offer-testing-roadmap` needs a concrete deliverable as its vehicle
- The user's current magnet exists but pulls no replies, or pulls freebie-hunters instead of buyers
- Anyone asks what they could possibly give away for free

## The bar the asset has to clear

Three tests, all of them, before an asset is worth building:

1. **Genuinely pay-worthy.** Would the prospect pay real money for this? Not a templated checklist, not a generic AI-written report. If it is a template, it is packed with real IP. A cheap-feeling freebie kills the offer and taints the brand behind it.
2. **Cheap to deliver at volume.** If 50 people say yes this month, can you deliver 50 without going under? The best magnets are produced once and reused, or generated per-prospect in minutes, not hours.
3. **Demonstrates competence.** Receiving it should make buying from you feel like the natural next step, because the magnet is a live demonstration of the thing you sell.

Tension between 1 and 2 is the whole design problem. You resolve it with scoping and staging (below), never by shipping something thin.

## The three types of free value

Every good magnet is one of these. Name which one yours is; if it fits none, it is probably a bribe, not a magnet:

1. **Reveal a problem.** An asset that exposes a problem the prospect has: a benchmark, a scorecard, a teardown, "the 7 mistakes {ICP} make when {doing X}". The diagnosis is the value; your service is the cure.
2. **Trial or sample of the solution.** A piece of the actual work, free: a rebuilt slice, a done-for-you unit, a working sample. The strongest type, because it gives away the most. The question to push on: how MUCH can you give away?
3. **One step of a multi-step.** Your service has a natural sequence; give the first step free (the plan, the setup, the first deliverable). Real standalone value, and the prospect is already one step into your process.

### The vehicle menu

Common vehicles, each with the interest-check ask it naturally carries:

- **Case study breakdown**: how a peer got the result. "want to see how they did it?"
- **Ideas / plan**: tailored forward-looking value. "would that be useful?"
- **Rebuilt sample**: a slice of their asset, done properly. "want me to show you the before and after?"
- **Benchmark / comparison**: their number against a peer group. "want the full comparison?"
- **Competitive intel**: what their top competitors do that they don't. "want the summary?"
- **Data / research piece**: built once, reused for the whole list. "want the report?"
- **Quick-win work**: a small scoped piece of real work, free. "want me to send them over?"
- **Specific-to-them observation**: two or three sentences of genuine analysis, no artifact beyond the email itself. Best for high-value deals.

Vehicles that are dead on arrival: the generic free audit and the free consultation. Both signal effort and sales process, both are saturated, and both ask for commitment before any value lands. If the value genuinely is diagnostic, package it as a specific named deliverable (a teardown, a scorecard, a breakdown) instead of calling it an audit.

### Scoring candidates

When several candidates survive the three tests, score each 1 to 5 on: cheap to deliver at volume, genuinely pay-worthy, demonstrates competence, and unique versus what the prospect's inbox already gets. Fifteen out of twenty or better is worth building; below that, rework or drop. The "unique" line has to be honest: if every competitor in the space runs free audits, an audit-shaped magnet caps at 1 or 2 no matter how good yours is, and that alone is a reason to pick a different vehicle.

## The Built-For-You shape (the workhorse framing)

The single most reliable framing for a magnet-backed cold email. Three beats, in order:

1. **The claim, in the past tense:** "we just built {asset} for {COMPANY}". Not "I could build", not "would you like me to build". Built. Past tense. The thing exists. That is what separates this from every hypothetical pitch in their inbox: there is nothing to agree to, nothing to schedule, no work on their side. Someone already did something specifically for them.
2. **The payoff:** one line on what it gets them, the pain skipped or the outcome reached. This is the angle from your testing roadmap.
3. **The interest check:** "want me to show you?" or "want to see it?" That is the entire CTA. The thing exists, so the only question is whether they want to look at it.

Put together, the skeleton:

> {first name}, we just built {asset} for {COMPANY} to {payoff} without {frustration}. want me to show you?

The past-tense claim must be honest. Two legitimate ways to make it true at volume:

- **Actually pre-build it** from public inputs. If the asset can be generated from their domain, their careers page, their public listings, you can genuinely build it for every prospect on the list before sending.
- **Build the demonstrative version.** You genuinely built the system, the framework, or the sample once, configured for their situation type, and the per-prospect run happens on reply. "we just set up a screening agent for roles like {COMPANY}'s" is honest if the agent exists and was pre-run on that role type. What you can never do is claim bespoke findings you have not generated: "we analysed YOUR product and found three issues" is a lie at volume, and it reads like one.

**Always write the Offer First twin as well.** Every magnet gets both frames for the testing roadmap:

> "if I put together {asset} to {payoff}, would that be useful?"

Conditional, forward-looking, zero claim to existing work. Some markets prefer being asked; some prefer the fait accompli. Test both, per `/offer-testing-roadmap`.

## Design rules that make or break the asset

**Scope a free service-sample to a tight, repeatable, cost-capped unit.** Do not offer the whole thing free. "We will rebuild your entire onboarding flow, free" reads as too good to be true (which hurts trust) and is uncapped cost for you. Instead pick a fixed slice: the opening 30 seconds, one page, one role, one workflow. Choose the slice that proves the entire offer in one move, the one that shows every part of your craft at once. Scoping raises believability AND caps your delivery cost at the same time.

**Stage the delivery.** When the magnet is real per-prospect work, never promise the full version to the whole list. A wave of yeses would bankrupt your hours. Lead with a lightweight version delivered in the reply (two or three specific findings, a quick teardown), and reserve the full deliverable for prospects who engage past the first exchange. The prospect never sees the tiers; you just control the depth.

**Never gate it.** No forms, no "enter your email", no 20-field intake. The maximum ask of the prospect is a reply, maybe with one or two data points. Gating a cold-email magnet kills the reply rate that was the entire point.

**Do not gate the offer on an asset the prospect may not have.** If your magnet needs an input half the list lacks (an existing video, a live ad account, a current careers page), you have silently disqualified half the list. Unify the source: "from your existing {asset} if you have one, built from your site and brand if you don't." The deliverable stays the same either way.

**Protect the IP in the delivery, not the pitch.** Never write hedges into the copy: "the thinking, not the full build", "an outline, not the real thing". Announcing what they are NOT getting guts the pull. Pitch a concrete, genuinely good version of the thing; control how deep you actually go when you deliver.

**Do not dangle the finding.** Say what you built, not what it revealed. "we put together a ranking of your local competitors. want to see it?" works. "we ranked your local competitors and the top spot is not who you'd expect..." over-promises, reads gimmicky, and burns trust when the reveal underwhelms.

**Pick the fastest honest delivery mode.** Instant beats promised. If the asset can be computed from a public input, pre-build it so it can land the moment they say yes. If not, a ready-made asset handed over in the reply. A live walkthrough only where the vehicle genuinely needs one. Whatever the mode, the cold email itself stays a pure interest check: the asset, and anything like a link to it, arrives in the reply after they say yes, never in the first email.

## Steps (what I actually do)

1. **Read `profiles/profile.yaml`.** The service, the proof points, the case studies, the ICP's pains. If it does not exist, run `/icp-builder` first.
2. **Pick the angle.** From `/offer-testing-roadmap` if a roadmap exists; otherwise the sharpest single pain in the profile. The magnet delivers value against that one angle, not against everything the company does.
3. **Choose the free-value type and vehicle.** Which of the three types fits? Which vehicle can this user actually produce cheaply at volume? Bias toward samples of the real work (type 2) when deliverable, then problem-revealers (type 1), then first-steps (type 3).
4. **Scope it.** Define the fixed, repeatable unit. Write down what the lightweight reply-stage version is and what the full engaged-prospect version is.
5. **Run the three tests.** Pay-worthy? Deliverable at 50 yeses? Demonstrates the actual service? If any fail, redesign before writing a word of copy.
6. **Write both framings.** The Built-For-You (past tense, payoff, "want me to show you?") and the Offer First twin. No links, no mention of calls or scheduling, CTA is the interest check and nothing else.
7. **Define the delivery flow.** What exactly happens on a yes: what goes in the first reply, what the escalation for engaged prospects is, and who produces it.

## The pre-send checklist

Before the magnet-backed email goes anywhere near a campaign:

- The past-tense claim is honest for every single prospect on the list, not just the ones you spot-checked
- The asset passes the pay-worthy test on a real example, not in theory
- The delivery flow is written down: what lands in the reply, what the escalation is, who produces it
- The copy contains no link, no mention of a call, and no time commitment; the CTA is the interest check alone
- Someone has actually produced one full sample of the asset end to end, and it is good

## Examples (all fictional)

**Type 2, sample of the work. Osprey Sound, an audio post-production studio.**
Asset: a free remix of the opening 30 seconds of a prospect's latest published video. Tight slice, repeatable, and 30 seconds still shows the full craft.

> {first name}, we just remixed the opening of {COMPANY}'s latest brand film, so the voiceover cuts through instead of fighting the music. want me to show you the before and after?

Delivery: the remixed clip goes out in the reply. The full-video treatment is reserved for prospects who come back after hearing it.

**Type 1, reveal a problem. Lanternhill, a careers-page optimisation consultancy.**
Asset: a hiring-page teardown, pre-built from each prospect's public careers page.

> {first name}, we just put together a teardown of {COMPANY}'s careers page, the three places applicants drop off before they ever hit apply. want to see it?

Pre-computable from public input, so the past tense is honest for the entire list.

**Type 3, first step of a multi-step. Quaymark, a warehouse automation integrator.**
Asset: the phase-one automation plan they normally build in week one of a paid engagement.

> {first name}, if I put together the phase-one automation plan for a site like {COMPANY}'s, the one we usually start paid projects with, would that be useful?

Offer First frame here, because the plan needs a short exchange of inputs, so the conditional framing is the honest one. Its Built-For-You twin uses a demonstrative version built for their site type.

## Common mistakes

- **Proposing a magnet the user cannot deliver.** "Free audit" from someone who has never produced one is a disaster on the first yes. Confirm delivery capacity before writing copy.
- **The meeting as the magnet.** A meeting is the ask, not the gift. The magnet is what they get before any meeting is ever mentioned.
- **Thin freebies.** A generic checklist falls completely flat with expert buyers, who feel (correctly) that they already know it. Specificity is what pulls; if it is generic, it is not a magnet.
- **Fake past tense.** Claiming bespoke findings you never generated. It is dishonest, and prospects smell it. Pre-build for real, or use the demonstrative version, or use the Offer First frame.
- **Announcing the limits.** "Just a quick rough version, nothing fancy" strips the value in one line. Scope silently.
- **Gating behind forms or calls.** Every field and every scheduling step between the yes and the value costs you most of your replies.
- **Whole-thing-free offers.** Uncapped cost for you, and too-good-to-be-true for them. Scope to the slice.
- **Teasing the result instead of naming the asset.** The artifact existing is the pull. Dangling a mystery finding is a gimmick.
