# 02: Offers and Strategy (condensed)

Most cold email advice is about copy. Most cold email failure is about the offer. Copy cannot fix a bad offer; it can only sharpen a winning one. This page is the condensed doctrine; the full system lives in the skills linked at the bottom.

## The diagnosis order (memorize this)

When a campaign is flat, diagnose in this order and never skip a step:

1. **Offer value.** Would the ideal prospect genuinely want this? Ask the brutal question: why would the person reading this say no? If you can't answer cleanly, the offer isn't sharp enough. Fix this first; nothing downstream matters until it's fixed.
2. **Framing.** The offer is good but the email is confusing, wordy, or buries it. That's a copy-clarity fix, not an offer change.
3. **Audience.** Offer and framing are right but pointed at the wrong list.

And the rule that sits above all three: **volume is fuel, not the fix.** Sending more of an offer nobody wants just gets you ignored at scale, and trains spam filters against you while it happens. When results are thin, the answer is almost never "more".

Also read WHO replies, not just how many. An offer that pulls freebie-hunters, networkers, and competitors is not working, however busy the inbox looks. Buyer-pull beats reply volume.

## The one principle: free value first

**The one who wins cold email is the one who gives away the most genuine value for free, upfront.**

A stranger with no trust and ten other pitches in their inbox needs a reason to reply that costs them nothing and risks nothing. So the question is never "what do I sell them". It's "how much genuinely pay-worthy value am I willing to hand over before they've given me anything?"

Two consequences:

- The free thing must be something they would actually PAY for. A templated PDF or generic AI report is worse than nothing: it signals what working with you is like.
- If any free element exists (a trial, a free sample of the work, a free first step), it belongs in every variant you send. Phrase it "on the house" or "no cost"; the literal word "free" is a spam trigger.

## Where offers come from: distance from the point of sale

Every offer sits on a line measured by how far it is from the thing you sell:

1. **The service itself** (closest, best): free, on trial, on performance, or guaranteed. Strongest, because it gives away the most and the yeses arrive already wanting the conversation. Lead here whenever a real risk-reversal or genuinely low-commitment entry point exists. Ask yourself the extracting question: "what could I guarantee, or give away, and stand behind it?" Most people undersell what they could offer.
2. **A deliverable tied to the service** (middle): a plan, a teardown, a case study walkthrough, a built asset. This is lead magnet territory, and it's where you go when the service can't carry a no-brainer on its own. The free deliverable IS the risk reversal.
3. **An unrelated incentive** (furthest, almost never right): gift cards and giveaways buy the wrong yes and teach you nothing.

Start close to the sale, move out only when forced. And note what dies on cold traffic: the raw commodity pitch ("we do web design, interested?") with no risk reversal and no entry point. Cold email is demand generation; those people were not searching for you.

Concrete beats process: "can I send you the playbook?" works, "can I give you a free consultation?" doesn't. An audit signals work; a consultation reads as a sales call in disguise, because it is one.

## The two base frames

Every email in this system is built on one of two bases (full mechanics in `03-copywriting.md`):

- **Offer First:** state the offer as a value question, ask if it's useful. "If I put together {deliverable} for {{COMPANY}}, would that be useful?"
- **Built For You:** the asset already exists, past tense. "Just built {{COMPANY}} {specific asset}. Want me to send it over?" Only usable when you can genuinely produce it for everyone who says yes.

Hold the frame steady and vary the ANGLE: the specific pain, outcome, or mechanism the offer is about. Which angle the market bites on is the thing you're actually testing.

## The roadmap: one offer per week, angles as the variable

A campaign is not a launch, it's a search, and almost nobody guesses the winning angle first time. The teams that win aren't the best first-guessers; they're the ones whose week-two offer is already written when week one's dies.

So before launch, build a roadmap:

- A queue of genuinely distinct offer ANGLES (different pain, different outcome, different metric), not five wordings of one offer. Early on, wording tests spread your volume thin and teach you nothing.
- **Default cadence: one offer per week/campaign.** Spray-many-angles-at-once is only for the earliest discovery phase.
- Kill rules decided in advance, by sends rather than by calendar, so a dead angle dies on evidence instead of hope. Judge on interested reply rate: 20%+ of replies interested is great, 10%+ good, under 5% means change something upstream.
- The roadmap also feeds the sequence: step 3 of every campaign is a NEW angle on the same list (see `04-sequence-structure.md`), so each campaign tests two angles for one list cost.
